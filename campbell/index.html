Keeping Your Campbell Water Heater Running Longer

Many early failures trace back to maintenance that never happened, and the habits that prevent them are easy. Flush the tank once a year to clear sediment, get the anode rod checked every few years, and test the relief valve annually so it doesn't seize. Set the thermostat close to 120 degrees to reduce scald risk and slow scale buildup. If you have a tankless unit, descale it yearly where the water is hard so the heat exchanger stays clean.

Age matters too. Most tank water heaters last 8 to 12 years, tankless units often reach 20 with regular descaling, and heat pump models usually run 10 to 15. If your heater is past that window and showing symptoms such as rusty hot water, rumbling during heating, moisture around the base, or hot water that runs out sooner than it used to, a planned replacement is nearly always less expensive and much easier on you than an emergency one after the tank lets go.

Minor issues don't stay minor for long. Consider a spent anode rod: swapping it out is a quick, inexpensive repair, but once it's gone, the water starts eating the tank steel, and suddenly a minor fix becomes a full replacement. That's why we ask homeowners to save our number in their phone and call at the first hint of trouble. Catching it early saves time and money and spares you a surprise cleanup.

Hot Water Headaches Campbell Homeowners Face

Water heaters in and around Campbell deal with a familiar set of stresses. Depending on the neighborhood and the water source, minerals can be part of the picture: calcium and magnesium drop out as sediment on the tank floor, insulating the water from the burner, which produces the rumbling and popping you may hear when the heater fires. In tankless units, the same minerals build scale inside the heat exchanger, and that's why regular descaling matters so much where the water is hard.

Then there's age. When the sacrificial anode rod is gone, often well before the tank itself looks old, the steel begins corroding from within, and a slow leak at the base is usually the first visible sign. Homes here also run on a mix of fuel types, natural gas and propane units in some homes and electric in others, which means we service both sides: thermocouples, burners, and venting on gas units, and elements, thermostats, and breakers on electric ones.

Code requirements add another layer. Swapping out an older heater frequently means bringing the install up to current code: an expansion tank if the water system is closed, a relief valve drain line that terminates correctly, venting that drafts properly on gas units, and a drain pan or seismic straps where required. Homes on well water have their own issues, including extra sediment, iron staining, and the sulfur odor that sometimes shows up when certain water chemistry reacts with a standard anode rod. Every one of these problems is fixable, and most cost far less to address early. If something seems off, a diagnostic visit from our team can keep a small problem from turning into a flooded floor.
